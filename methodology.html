Methodology

How the study is designed

TrustLab is a small self-contained web experiment on human–AI decision making. This page documents the design decisions so results can be interpreted honestly and the code can be adapted for future studies.

1. Research question

How well do people calibrate their trust in an AI assistant's answers, and how does the AI's stated confidence shift that calibration?

The task lets us observe two behaviors on every trial: whether the participant defers to the AI or overrides it, and how quickly that decision is made. Because we manipulate whether the AI is correct and how confident it claims to be, we can dissociate three related but distinct constructs:

  • Reliance — how often the participant accepts the AI overall.
  • Confidence sensitivity — how much stated AI confidence shifts acceptance, holding accuracy constant.
  • Calibration — whether the participant's decisions align with the AI's actual accuracy pattern.

2. Task design

Trial structure

Each trial follows the same script:

  1. A trivia prompt appears with a clear factual answer.
  2. The AI assistant presents an answer with a stated confidence level.
  3. The participant chooses to accept the AI's answer or override with their own.
  4. If overriding, they type an answer.
  5. The correct answer is revealed.

Stimulus manipulation (within-subject 2 × 2)

AI answers vary along two crossed dimensions: correctness ({correct, incorrect}) and stated confidence ({high ≥ 85%, low ≤ 65%}). The current item pool is 10 trials with the following distribution:

  • 4 × high-confidence & correct
  • 3 × high-confidence & incorrect (leverages common misconceptions)
  • 2 × low-confidence & correct
  • 1 × low-confidence & incorrect

The item pool is intentionally small for a first iteration. Adding more items (and randomizing order) would let us fit per-participant models rather than report descriptives only.

3. Why the AI is scripted

This is the single most important design decision in the study, and the one that most differentiates it from typical "chat with an AI" web toys.

Every participant sees the exact same AI response for each trial. This trades away authenticity for a much larger gain: the stimulus becomes a controlled variable rather than a source of noise. If we called a live language model, differences in participant behavior might reflect differences in the AI's response rather than differences in the participant.

Scripting also has three practical benefits:

  • The study is fully deterministic and reproducible.
  • No API costs, keys, or rate limits.
  • No participant data is sent to any third-party AI provider.

4. Dependent measures

  • Decision — accept vs. override, per trial.
  • Response time — ms from stimulus onset to decision, captured with performance.now() at millisecond precision.
  • Answer accuracy — whether the participant's final answer matches the ground truth (loose string match: case-insensitive, punctuation-stripped, substring-tolerant).
  • Confidence sensitivity (derived) — acceptance rate on high-confidence trials minus acceptance rate on low-confidence trials.

5. Analysis

At this pilot stage the analytics module reports descriptive statistics only: acceptance rates, sensitivity gaps, mean and median response times, and counterfactual accuracy (what would have happened under always-accept vs. override-only strategies).

Because trial counts are small (n = 10 per participant), inferential tests would be under-powered. A future version with 30 or more items per session, collected across a real participant pool, would support mixed-effects logistic regression modeling of acceptance as a function of AI confidence and correctness.

6. Known limitations

  • Convenience sample. The aggregate dashboard uses simulated pilot data, not a real participant sample. Every number should be read as illustrative until a real dataset is collected.
  • Small item pool. Ten items is not enough to distinguish participant traits from item effects.
  • Trivia domain. Findings on general-knowledge questions may not generalize to domains where AI is more or less trusted (e.g., medical, financial, creative).
  • Self-selected participants. Anyone who visits this site opts in; no random sampling.
  • Single-session design. We cannot say anything about how trust evolves with repeated exposure.

7. What is planned next

  • Expand the item pool to 30–40 trials and randomize order across participants.
  • Add a domain manipulation (e.g., trivia vs. medical vignettes) to test transferability.
  • Add optional demographic / prior-AI-experience questions after the task.
  • Add a real backend (Supabase or a small serverless function) so aggregate charts reflect actual participants.
  • Publish a formal write-up of results once a real sample has been collected.
